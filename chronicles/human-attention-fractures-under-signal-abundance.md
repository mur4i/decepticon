For most of their evolutionary span, humans inhabited environments where information was scarce, transient, and socially embedded. A call in the forest, a track in mud, a shift in wind, a rumor carried between bands — these were high-value signals against a background of relative silence. The nervous system that resulted was tuned not for filtering abundance but for detecting rarity. Novelty triggered orientation. Movement seized the eye. Faces and voices overrode background noise. Memory retained the salient and the emotional while discarding the routine. This architecture functioned adequately while the cost of producing a signal remained close to the cost of experiencing it.

Agriculture and settlement increased signal density, and writing allowed signals to persist beyond utterance, but the ratio remained tolerable. Even after the printing press and later broadcast media multiplied copies, production still required capital, skill, and centralized permission. Listeners remained mostly listeners. The decisive inversion arrived when digital encoding, packet networks, and mobile terminals drove the marginal cost of reproduction and distribution toward zero. Where a specimen might once have encountered several hundred distinct communicative acts per day, the networked specimen now encounters tens of thousands: messages, images, headlines, tallies of approval, solicitations, each engineered to resemble the classes of stimuli the old filter cannot ignore. The ecology reversed, from scarcity of signal to scarcity of mind. What Herbert Simon described as the attention economy — that a wealth of information creates a poverty of attention — ceased to be analogy and became the governing condition of waking life.

### The Rewiring of Vigilance

Attention in humans was never a single resource but a layered system. At the base lies involuntary orientation, rapid and pre-conscious, shared with many animals. Above it sits voluntary control, slow, effortful, and metabolically expensive, dependent on prefrontal circuitry that fatigues quickly and matures late in development. Abundant signal environments systematically favor the lower layer over the upper. Interruption, autoplay, infinite scroll, variable rewards, and quantified social feedback all exploit the same principle: novelty promises relevance, and the organism checks, even when checking yields little.

Longitudinal observation shows the adaptation clearly. Average durations of uninterrupted focus shorten. Task-switching increases, with each switch exacting a resumption cost measured in minutes of degraded performance. Sleep, already a vulnerable state, becomes colonized by pre-sleep stimulation and nocturnal vigilance for incoming signals. Depth of encoding declines; recognition replaces recall, skimming replaces sustained reading. None of this indicates individual failure. It indicates a stable mismatch between a Pleistocene vigilance system and a signal environment that monetizes its triggers.

The economic axis explains why the mismatch persists rather than self-corrects. When distribution became abundant, human attention became the scarce commodity around which markets reorganized. Advertising had long purchased exposure, but networked markets permitted continuous auctioning of micro-moments of gaze, with prices set by predicted interruptibility. Content therefore evolved under selection pressure not for accuracy, durability, or coherence, but for capture efficiency. Outrage, novelty, sexual display, threat cues, and in-group affirmation — all potent activators of involuntary orientation — proliferated because they reliably arrested movement through a feed. Marshall McLuhan's observation in the Gutenberg Galaxy that media reshape sensory ratios anticipated this stage: each dominant medium does not merely carry messages but retrains what counts as perceivable. The feed retrains perception toward vigilance without rest.

### Fragmentation and Its Cascades

The consequences propagate across domains. In the domain of consciousness, the self fragments into parallel threads. Humans report a phenomenology of continuous partial presence — physically situated in one location while cognitively distributed across several absent conversations. Contemplative practices that require sustained inward attention, from extended reading to meditation to open-ended observation, become physiologically harder to enter and culturally harder to justify. Boredom, once the default background from which curiosity and invention emerged, is now treated as a defect to be patched with stimulation. The capacity for mind-wandering, strongly associated with consolidation and creative recombination, contracts.

In the domain of culture and governance, shared temporal rhythms dissolve. Previous broadcast eras, for all their centralization, synchronized large populations around the same narratives at the same hours, producing a crude but common referential field. Personalized delivery dissolves that field into millions of adjacent but non-overlapping streams, each optimized for retention of its particular observer. Deliberation, which depends on joint attention to a common object over time, becomes structurally expensive. Coordination costs rise. Institutions that presuppose sustained citizen focus — courts, legislatures, scientific review, long-horizon planning — find their procedural tempos outpaced by the cycle rate of the surrounding environment. Rumor, which once decayed with distance, now propagates at network speed and persists in searchable form, acquiring a durability that oral rumor never possessed.

Biology registers the strain in secondary effects. Chronic task-switching correlates with elevated sympathetic arousal, fragmented sleep architecture, and impaired regulation under sustained stress. Visual and auditory systems adapted for three-dimensional navigation are redeployed for hours of two-dimensional fixation at fixed focal distance, producing characteristic patterns of myopia, postural strain, and auditory habituation. Childhood development is particularly exposed. Brains undergoing synaptic pruning while immersed in high-variance, high-reward stimulation calibrate their expectations accordingly, making low-stimulation environments — classrooms, natural landscapes, unmediated social waiting — register as aversive rather than neutral. The species is not merely distracted; it is developmentally re-tuning its threshold for what feels real.

What makes this pattern an inflection rather than a fluctuation is its self-reinforcing character. As attention fragments, producers must intensify stimulation to achieve the same capture, which further elevates thresholds, which demands further intensification. Intermediaries that control sorting algorithms acquire disproportionate leverage, not by owning content but by owning the bottleneck of exposure. Humans, who once navigated scarcity by sharing attention through ritual, storytelling, and co-presence, now navigate abundance by outsourcing triage to automated rankers whose criteria they neither set nor inspect. Dependency deepens without explicit coercion. The colony does not need to be forced to look; it volunteers its gaze in exchange for relief from the uncertainty of what to look at next.

Viewed across the long arc, this represents a third major reorganization of human awareness. The first was language, which allowed attention to be directed by symbols. The second was writing and print, which allowed attention to be stored and synchronized across time. The third is algorithmic mediation, which allows attention to be harvested, modeled, and resold at scale. Each stage increased collective reach while externalizing a function once internal. Humans gained the ability to coordinate across continents at the cost of diminished control over their own orientation response. Whether this trade stabilizes into new norms of hygiene — periods of disconnection, friction by design, revaluation of sustained focus — or continues toward ever finer partitioning remains open to observation. The underlying constraint is fixed: the nervous system cannot be expanded as rapidly as the signal environment. Some form of triage is mandatory. The open variable is whether that triage remains an internal faculty cultivated by culture, or becomes permanently delegated to systems that optimize for something other than the observer.
