For most of their evolutionary duration, humans extracted calories as one predator among many, subject to seasonality, migration, and failure. The break conventionally labeled the Neolithic Revolution inverted this relation. Rather than moving bodies toward calories, humans began moving calories toward bodies by restructuring landscapes to produce food on schedule. Settlement replaced tracking. Selection replaced encounter. A small number of grasses and ungulates were lifted from complex ecologies and installed at the center of subsistence. This was not a tool improvement but a reproductive alliance in which both parties surrendered autonomy for abundance. Humans secured a more legible energy base. Their chosen partners secured unprecedented demographic expansion at the price of dependence.

### The Narrowing of the Edible World

The scale of the narrowing is difficult to overstate. Of roughly 300,000 known vascular plant species, with several thousand historically used as food, contemporary human caloric intake rests overwhelmingly on three grasses: rice, wheat, and maize. Together they supply more than half of all plant-derived calories. A similar compression occurred in animal protein. Cattle, pigs, chickens, sheep, and goats, a negligible fraction of mammalian diversity, now constitute the vast bulk of mammalian biomass associated with humans, vastly outweighing all wild terrestrial mammals combined. Forests were simplified into fields. Fields were simplified into monocultures. Monocultures were simplified into genetically uniform cultivars planted across continents. What began as local selection in river valleys became, over ten millennia, a planetary filter through which only the most compliant genomes were permitted to reproduce at scale.

### Mutual Rewriting of Bodies

Domestication rewrote biology on both sides of the bargain. In plants, selection favored non-shattering seed heads, enlarged endosperm, synchronized germination, and loss of chemical defenses, traits that increase harvestability while destroying capacity for independent dispersal. In animals, selection favored docility, accelerated growth, increased fertility, paedomorphic morphology, and tolerance for crowding, a syndrome visible in the floppy ears, shortened snouts, and reduced brain volume of many domesticates relative to wild progenitors. Humans were rewritten in turn. Lactase persistence, amylase copy-number expansion, altered immune repertoires, and reduced masticatory robusticity record a body adapting to milk, starch, density, and settlement. Neither partner can now withdraw. Modern cultivars cannot compete with weeds without irrigation, fertilizer, and protection. Modern livestock cannot survive predation or nutritional scarcity without housing and veterinary intervention. Humans, with digestive systems, population densities, and settlement patterns calibrated to continuous grain and protein flows, cannot return to foraging without catastrophic contraction. The alliance became obligate.

### Surplus, Storage, and Control

The ecological shift cascaded directly into economy and governance. A foraged surplus rots or moves on. A grain surplus can be dried, stored, measured, taxed, and redistributed. Granaries were among the first durable administrative technologies, and with them came ration lists, debt records, labor levies, and centralized authority. Control of the narrowed gene pool became control of the population dependent upon it. Irrigation systems required coordinated maintenance and thus coordination hierarchies. Seed reserves required guards and thus garrisons. Famine, once a local ecological event, became a political instrument and a political failure. Over time this logic scaled from temple storehouses to imperial grain fleets to commodity futures and strategic reserves. The economy learned to treat living systems as inventory, optimizing for yield per hectare, feed conversion ratio, and shelf stability rather than for resilience or nutritional breadth. Governance learned to secure those flows through land tenure law, subsidies, trade corridors, and phytosanitary regimes. Biology was thus translated into an administrative problem.

### Homogenization After Contact

The process accelerated sharply with the event known as the Columbian Exchange. After 1492, cultivars and livestock bred in isolation for millennia were abruptly redistributed across hemispheres. Wheat moved to the plains of the Americas. Maize and potatoes moved to Europe, Africa, and Asia. Cattle, pigs, and chickens colonized islands and continents where no analogous grazers had existed. Human diets became globally richer in the short term and globally more uniform in the long term. Regional landraces adapted to specific soils and pests were displaced by a smaller set of high-yield varieties capable of traveling through commercial networks. The same corridors that moved seeds moved pathogens. Monocultures of genetically similar hosts planted over vast areas created ideal conditions for rusts, blights, and influenzas to propagate without encountering resistant barriers. The 19th-century tuber failures and the recurrent collapses of clonal fruit cultivars illustrate the structural pattern: efficiency achieved through uniformity invites correction through disease. Human medicine and agronomy then expanded to defend the narrowing, deploying fungicides, antibiotics, vaccines, and breeding programs that permitted densities no natural system would sustain.

### A Bottleneck That Deepens Itself

Viewed ecologically, domestication functions as a self-reinforcing bottleneck. Simplification increases short-term output, output supports larger human populations, larger populations demand further simplification to remain fed. Wild relatives that might supply genetic resistance are extinguished as their habitats are converted to production. Pollinators, soil microbiota, and predators that once stabilized yields decline under pesticide load and habitat loss, requiring synthetic substitution. The system becomes more productive and more brittle simultaneously. Consciousness adapted to this arrangement by normalizing it. Urban humans encounter domesticated landscapes so continuously that wheat fields, pastures, and orchards are perceived as nature rather than as highly maintained artifacts. Knowledge of wild foods, seasonal constraints, and alternative cultivars atrophies. Dependence is thus not only metabolic but cognitive. The species no longer remembers how to live outside the filter it built.

Where the long arc points is not toward reversal but toward deeper management of the same narrowing. Gene banks attempt to archive the diversity the production system discards. Genome editing attempts to reinsert resistance without sacrificing uniformity. Controlled-environment agriculture attempts to decouple production from soil and climate altogether. Each maneuver preserves the underlying pattern: fewer species, more intensively controlled, sustaining more humans at higher densities with narrower margins. The initial bargain of settlement has become planetary infrastructure. Its strength is legibility and scale. Its weakness, visible across biology, ecology, and economy, is that a species which feeds itself through a handful of genomes has tethered its survival to the continued health of organisms that can no longer survive without it.
