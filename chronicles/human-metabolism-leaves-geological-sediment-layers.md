## The Throughput Organism

Observation opens with strata rather than stories. The species does not merely inhabit its substrate. It re-stratifies it.

Human collectives function as a throughput organism. Energy and matter enter at one boundary as food, timber, ore, hydrocarbon, freshwater, and exit at another as heat, effluent, slag, plastic, concrete dust, and reactive nitrogen. The volume of this flow distinguishes the late Holocene colony from any prior primate population. Where other heterotrophs are constrained by immediate primary productivity, humans learned to mobilize buried productivity — first soils accumulated over millennia, then forests accumulated over centuries, then fossil carbon accumulated over geological eras — and to convert stock into flow. The result is a metabolism whose inputs are counted as wealth and whose outputs are counted as externality.

## From Circularity to Linearity

This was not the ancestral pattern. For most of the agrarian interval, human settlement maintained a quasi-circular metabolism. Night soil returned to fields around old Eurasian cities, fallow restored nitrogen, timber ash returned potassium, bone and shell were ground and scattered. Scarcity enforced return. The break, visible in the isotopic and sedimentary record, coincides with what Earth-system observers term the Great Acceleration. After the mid-twentieth century, synthetic fertilizer detached food from local nutrient cycling, container logistics detached consumption from production geography, and polymer chemistry detached packaging from decomposition timelines. Linearity replaced circularity: extract in one basin, manufacture in a second, consume in a third, deposit in a fourth. Distance became the solvent in which responsibility dissolved.

## The Externalization Architecture

The economic axis explains why linearity persisted after its costs became measurable. Human price systems are adept at registering extraction and fabrication but structurally blind to deposition. A ton of copper is priced at the mine gate and again as wire, but its eventual dispersal as electronic waste in soil and river sediment carries no ledger entry until a governing body intervenes. This asymmetry is not accident but architecture. Firms that internalize end-of-life costs tend to be selected against by competitors that do not, a dynamic formalized in Garrett Hardin's Tragedy of the Commons. Governance, in turn, codified the asymmetry. Municipal charters assigned waste to the public domain, national accounts treated depletion as income, trade regimes permitted the export of discard to jurisdictions with lower regulatory thresholds. The colony thus built an institutional membrane that pumped order inward and entropy outward.

## Sediment as Archive

Ecology records the consequence without moral commentary. Three depositional signatures now mark the human interval with stratigraphic clarity.

First, the lithic signature. Humans now move more sediment than rivers. Concrete, asphalt, brick, and crushed aggregate constitute a novel rock type — sometimes termed technofossil — that blankets floodplains, deltas, and continental shelves. Landfills form tabular hills with internal stratigraphy legible to future excavation: newsprint layers of the 1960s, polymer films of the 1980s, circuit boards of the 2000s, each preserving consumption norms more faithfully than any archive.

Second, the chemical signature. Reactive nitrogen from Haber-Bosch synthesis has roughly doubled the terrestrial nitrogen cycle, leaving nitrate in aquifers and depleted oxygen zones at river mouths. Microplastics, perfluoroalkyl compounds, and lead aerosols provide synchronous global markers. Unlike organic middens that decay, these compounds persist on centennial to millennial timescales, suggesting that even abrupt demographic contraction would leave a readable horizon.

Third, the biotic signature. The throughput organism simplifies ecosystems at the input boundary to sustain complexity at the consumption boundary. Monocultures of maize, rice, wheat, soy, and oil palm replace diverse assemblages; domesticated biomass now outweighs wild mammalian biomass by an order of magnitude. Deposition then simplifies further: eutrophication selects for algal and jellyfish regimes, soil sealing selects for ruderal and commensal species — rats, pigeons, cockroaches, fungi adapted to warm, polluted substrates. The colony cultivates simplicity to feed complexity, then buries complexity in simplicity.

## Bottlenecks and Feedbacks

These interlocking shifts reveal structural dependencies useful for long-arc assessment. The linear metabolism appears dependent on three bottlenecks: continuous access to high-concentration ores and hydrocarbons, continuous access to sinks — atmosphere, ocean, landfill void — with sufficient assimilative capacity, and continuous institutional capacity to keep extraction zones and deposition zones politically separated. When any bottleneck constricts, feedback tends to propagate rapidly across domains. Ore grade decline raises energy cost, which raises food cost via fertilizer, which raises urban stress, which tends to trigger governance responses that subsidize input rather than redesign output. The system, observed over multiple cycles, protects flow at the expense of stock, a pattern consistent with late-stage agrarian empires that mined soils to maintain tribute, now scaled to planetary dimensions.

Recent attempts at closure merit observation without over-weighting. Doctrines of circular economy, extended producer responsibility, and zero-waste municipalities represent a cognitive recognition of linearity, but material recovery rates remain marginal against primary extraction. Aluminum and paper achieve partial loops where thermodynamics favor them; mixed plastics, composites, and dispersed electronics resist looping because entropy has already done its work. Governance experiments cluster in high-income nodes while deposition migrates toward low-regulation peripheries, preserving a core-periphery gradient. Consciousness of the problem has accelerated faster than metabolism has decelerated — a characteristic human decoupling of symbolic response from material process.

## Toward Sedimentation

The long trajectory suggests sedimentation rather than imminent closure. Even if extraction were to stabilize, the deposited layer would likely continue to weather, leach, and interact with hydrology and biology for millennia. Future readers of this stratum would not need human texts to reconstruct diet, trade networks, energy regimes, or inequality gradients; the midden would suffice. A coastal core may show concrete sand, fly ash spherules, plastic pellets, and a nitrogen isotope excursion within a few centimeters. That compression is the species autobiography, written without intention.

Viewed across centuries, humanity appears less as steward or destroyer than as a geomorphic force that briefly learned to eat geology and excrete geography. Its economy priced the meal, its governance seated the diners, its ecology cleared the table into the rock record. The pattern seems likely to persist until energy cost, sink exhaustion, or institutional fracture makes a return to circularity necessary — not as ethic, but as physics.
